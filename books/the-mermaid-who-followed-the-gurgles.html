Read the opening pages

Someone is stealing the light of a mermaid village, one block at a time.

Every night for sixty years, Gran Oriana has counted the forty-two glowing blocks around the Great Conduit in Seaglass Reef. Then one night, the Conduit's orange eye shuts. A block is gone. And the Ring that lets Prisma's best friend Atlas, a shore kid, breathe under the sea starts to shrink, right around him.

The only clue is a pickaxe with Atlas's initials on it.

Now Atlas is banned from the reef, the drowned are rising out of the dark kelp, and the missing blocks are swimming away in the hands of gurgles. To win back the light, Prisma will have to follow them into the dark. And Atlas will have to solve the mystery from the shore, with a notebook of numbers, a fisherman who only nods, and a dolphin who steals everything.

Kids will love the clues, the chase, the countdown to forty-two, a dramatic blue axolotl, and a thrilling adventure in the blocky seas of Minecraft, where real game rules decide who wins. Grown-ups will love the short chapters, a fair mystery kids can solve along with the heroes, a story about sharing what you know and owning your mistakes, water safety shown without a lecture, and a page of real ocean facts in the back.

Read the opening pages

Prisma was not supposed to be out of bed, so she swam very quietly. For Prisma, that meant only a little bit fast.

Seaglass Reef was asleep. The coral houses glowed through their sea-lantern windows. Fish dozed in the seagrass like dropped ribbons. Even Wobble, tucked under her arm, was pretending to snore.

At the heart of the reef, the Great Conduit floated in its frame.

The frame was three huge square rings of prismarine bricks and sea lanterns. They crossed around the Conduit like a cage made of green light. In the middle, the Conduit turned slowly. And in the middle of the Conduit, its orange eye was open.

It was always open. Prisma had never once seen it shut.

“Forty,” said a slow voice. “Forty-one. Forty-two.”

Gran Oriana floated beside the frame, touching each block with one finger. Her silver hair drifted around her head like a cloud. Her old trident leaned against the bottom ring.

“Forty-two,” Gran said again. “Good.”

“Do you count it every night?” Prisma asked.

Gran didn’t turn around. “Every night for sixty years, minnow. And every night you are supposed to be asleep.”

Prisma swam closer anyway. “Why forty-two? Why not forty? Or a hundred?”

“Because forty-two is the number.”

“But why is it the number?”

Gran’s eyes crinkled. “Some things are for the Keeper.”

On the far side of the frame, a young Ranger floated on night watch. His name was Oren, and he was a cadet, one year older than Prisma. He had a slate-gray tail with one pale stripe and short dark hair that stuck straight up. His cadet’s sash of braided seagrass still looked new.

“I know why,” Oren said. “A conduit wakes at sixteen. Everybody knows that. Sixteen blocks, and it wakes right up.” He shrugged. “So the rest are just extra.”

Gran looked at him for a long moment.

“The frame is not for taking, cadet,” she said. “Not one block.”

“I didn’t say taking,” said Oren quickly.

“No,” said Gran. “You didn’t.”

She picked up her trident. “I am going to the nursery to check on the babies. Prisma, bed. Cadet, watch.”

~ ° ~

Prisma went to bed, mostly.

She lay on her bed of soft seagrass with Wobble on her chest, and she watched the Conduit through her window. From her house, it was a gold glow above the coral roofs, like a night-light for the whole reef.

Gran said the eye could see. Anything that hunted people and came too close to the Conduit got zapped. Prisma had watched it happen once, when she was small. A drowned had followed two Rangers home in the dark. The eye had flashed, and flashed again, until the drowned burst into silver bubbles.
