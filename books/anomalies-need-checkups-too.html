Read the opening pages

The puppy on the front step looks like any other patient. But on the hospital camera, it's standing in a snowstorm.

Every night at seven, Dulce Castillo, her little brother Tavo and their best friend Abe clock in to Pawprint Hospital, their own private server in a night-shift animal-hospital game. Dulce works the Night Window, Abe runs the cameras and the flash, and Tavo names every patient in three seconds flat.

The rules are simple. Check the tag. Check every patient three ways. And watch out for Lookalikes, the fakes that copy real patients. Let one inside, and the lights go out one by one.

So when a shivering white pup rings the bell at 11:42 PM, Dulce shutters it by the book. But it doesn't leave. It comes back every hour. Is it a fake, or does it need help?

At home, Dulce has a secret worry: her old dog Lentil has a senior checkup on Friday.

Read the opening pages

At 6:28 every evening, Papá’s work boots waited by the front door like two big black dogs who wanted a walk.

Dulce Castillo knew that because she checked. She checked a lot of things.

Papá came down the hall in his blue scrubs, humming an old bolero. He was big and gentle and a little sleepy. He worked nights as a vet nurse at the all-night animal hospital across town.

“Mija,” he said. “Catch.”

He tossed her something flat, and Dulce caught it against her chest. It was an old clipboard, scuffed at the corners, with H.C. scratched into the back.

“My very first clipboard from the hospital,” Papá said. “Every night shift needs a log. Write down what happens in your game, and I’ll read it when I get home.”

“What should we write?” asked Dulce. She pushed up her round red glasses and chose a pen from her lanyard of four: black, blue, green and red. Green was for beginnings.

“Times. Patients. Anything strange.” Papá sat on the bench to pull on his boots. “And remember the most important rule. Every patient gets a name and a kind look.”

“Tavo does the names,” said Dulce.

“Then you can do the looks,” said Papá.

The screen door squeaked, and Mom came in with Lentil on her red leash. Lentil was twelve years old. She had a gray muzzle, one ear up and one ear down, and a red bandana that never stayed straight.

She climbed the porch steps slowly, one at a time. On the last step, her back paw slipped.

It was only a small slip. Lentil caught herself and trotted inside, tail wagging, as if nothing had happened. But Dulce saw it, and something inside her went cold and quiet.

“Easy, old girl,” Papá said softly, rubbing her ears. “Friday is your big day, isn’t it?”

“What’s Friday?” asked Dulce.

“Her checkup with Dr. Wen. She goes twice a year now, because she’s a senior.” Papá smiled. “That just means an older dog.”

“Oh,” said Dulce. “Okay.”

She tapped her pen on her chin. Once, twice, three times. Then she wrote FRIDAY on the clipboard and didn’t write anything else.

Papá kissed the top of her high bun, right next to the pencil stuck through it. “Clock in for me, mija,” he said. At 6:30 on the dot, the screen door squeaked again, and he was gone.

+ + +

“Homework?” asked Mom.

“Done,” said Dulce.

“DONE!” yelled Tavo from the den. He was eight, with a lightning bolt shaved into his short hair and a gap where a front tooth used to be. His sneakers blinked red whenever he bounced, and he bounced a lot.

Mom held up one finger at a time. “Clock in at seven. Clock out at eight sharp. Usernames only, and private server only.”
