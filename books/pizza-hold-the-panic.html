A Funny Pizza Chapter Book for Kids 8-12

Read the opening pages

Hi! Welcome! Everything is fine! (It is not fine.)

Ten-year-old Mae Galanis isn't allowed behind the counter of her family's real pizza shop until she's fourteen. So after school, in the last red booth of Galanis House of Pizza, she finally gets her own username and clocks in to her friends' favorite pizza game. She has a fourteen-step plan to be the best new hire in history, and every step says "by myself."

Then her very first shift lands on the busiest four minutes of the day. The supplier is stuck at the dentist. The dough hits zero, and the whole restaurant stops. Mae flings a supply truck off a curve, gets zapped back to work by a manager who writes memos, and watches the stock board blink red while the clock ticks toward five.

There's one word Mae never wanted to say. It might be the only one that can save the shift.

Read the opening pages

In my family, you get a username when you turn ten. I turned ten on a Tuesday, and by Friday I had a list.

It was called How to Be the Best New Hire in History, and it had fourteen steps. Step one was “Learn every job.” Step two was “Do every job perfectly.” Steps three to fourteen were mostly the words “by myself,” underlined in different colors, and I was very proud of it.

The bell over the front door of Galanis House of Pizza went ding. On Fridays, the whole Shop smells like hot cheese and garlic. It’s hard not to walk in like you own the place.

“Booth,” said Mom from behind the counter, with the phone pinned to her shoulder. “Homework. Then pizza.”

“Yes, ma’am!” I said. “Hi! Welcome! I mean, hi, Mom.”

I was practicing.

The Back Booth is the last red booth in the Shop, right next to the kitchen door. From there, you can hear every order Mom calls back to Uncle Stav. The seats squeak, and the table is a little sticky no matter what anybody does. Taped inside, on an order pad, are the Booth Rules, written in Uncle Stav’s big capital letters. Rule two says USERNAMES ONLY, and I had picked mine on Tuesday night.

“MaydayMae,” I said, sliding in. “Pretty good, right?”

“You know what mayday means, right?” said Emilio.

He was already in the booth, drawing a pizza on a napkin. Emilio is eleven, tall and skinny, with a short black ponytail and paint on his fingers from art club. He has been my best friend since kindergarten, when he painted my shoe and called it a gift.

“It means May Day,” I said. “Like Mae. Day. It’s my day.”

“It’s what pilots yell on the radio when they need help,” he said.

“Well, I will never need help.”

Emilio held up his napkin. “Behold. Pepperoni, Thinking.”

“Why is the pepperoni thinking?”

“Art doesn’t explain itself, Mae.”

The kitchen door swung open, and Gracie came through it at full speed, making engine noises. She makes engine noises when she walks, when she runs, and once, during a spelling test. Gracie is ten like me, but shorter and much louder. She wears two braids with orange beads and her big brother’s hoodie, with the sleeves rolled up four times.

“Vroom,” she said, sliding into the booth like a car into a parking spot. “Is it today? It’s today! It’s your Roblox birthday!”

“It’s my first shift,” I said. “Officially.”

“So where’s Rafa?” asked Gracie.

Emilio pointed at a folded note on the table, so I opened it and read it out loud.

“Dentist. Back 4:40. Tight load.”

“That’s the longest note he has ever written,” said Gracie.
