Read the opening pages

My horse thinks she's a racehorse. She is nine sunflowers of ordinary speed. The pillagers didn't care either way.

When Peony sees the fastest horse in the village gallop past, she decides she's a racehorse. So ten-year-old Tansy Mayfield enters her very dramatic white mare in the Spring Fair's cross-country race, against her rival Priscilla and the speedy Majesty. Peony can't win on the flat. But the short way home crosses six old fences, and Peony jumps everything.

Then a pillager patrol walks onto the course. Five gray shapes with crossbows take the only bridge back to the fair, where the whole village is watching the finish, and where Tansy's six-year-old sister Goldie is guarding the village bell. A patrol walks right past you, unless you come within ten steps. Tansy is three sunflowers from winning, and she has a choice to make.

A heart-racing ranch adventure on the plains of Minecraft, where every rule of the patrol, the bell and the iron golem works just like in the game, and the danger is real but never graphic.

Read the opening pages

Peony decided she was a racehorse on a Monday, right after breakfast, and it was Majesty’s fault.

I was brushing Peony at the paddock fence. Spring brushing is the best brushing, because her winter hair comes out in white clouds. The clouds float off over the sunflowers while Peony leans into the brush with her eyes half shut. She smelled like warm dust and new grass.

Peony is my horse. She’s white from her nose to her tail, with a pale mane and dark eyes. She is very dramatic, and she thinks she’s a unicorn. She isn’t, so nobody tell her.

Then hooves came drumming down the lane.

It was Priscilla Vanderhoof on Majesty. Majesty is dark brown, with four white stockings and a white blaze, which is horse for a stripe down her face. She is the fastest horse in Cornflower Crossing, and she went past our fence like a thrown snowball. Priscilla sat very straight in her spotless boots and did not look at us at all.

Peony’s head came up, and both her ears pointed straight at Majesty.

Ears forward means I’m interested. Ears forward and quivering means I’m very interested, and I’m about to do something.

“Peony,” I said. “Steady on.”

She did not steady on. She spun away from my brush and galloped one whole circle of the paddock with her tail high like a flag. Then she skidded to a stop by the water trough. She stood there with her head up and her nostrils wide, looking down the lane where Majesty had gone.

“What is she doing?” I said.

“IMPORTANT!” My little sister Goldie climbed onto the fence in her pink leather cap. Goldie is six, and she says most things in capital letters. “IMPORTANT HORN CHECK!”

She leaned over and stared at the middle of Peony’s forehead.

“Still no horn,” she reported. “But it’s spring, and things sprout in spring.”

“Horns don’t sprout,” I said.

“Not with THAT attitude.”

Mom came out of the barn with a bucket of water in each hand. Mom always has a bucket in each hand. She looked at Peony, standing like a statue at the trough.

“That,” said Mom, “is the racehorse look.”

“Since when does Peony have a racehorse look?”

“Since about one sunflower ago.” Mom set down the buckets. “Water first, Tansy. Then whatever this is.”

So I did the water first, and then the hay. Then I picked out all four of Peony’s hooves with a stick. She held each one up politely, but her ears stayed on the lane the whole time.

Over by the round pen, Dad was up on a ladder, hammering. Dad is big and freckled, with a red beard full of sawdust. He was building Fence 5.1, which had a roof over Saffron’s favorite spot. That way she could rest in the shade and not wander.
