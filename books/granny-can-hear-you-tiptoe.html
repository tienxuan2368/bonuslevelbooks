Read the opening pages

Every midnight, something in Granny's locked attic moans, clanks and gallops. And Granny can hear you tiptoe.

Juno, Fritz and their cousin Santi have five days at Gravelly House, the creakiest house on Tumbledown Hill. Granny Gravelly is very tall, very strict, and hears EVERY sound: a spoon dropped two floors down, a whisper under a blanket. Her rule is simple. Not a peep.

At midnight, the attic above their beds comes alive with chains and hooves and thunder. The attic door has three locks, and Granny has the keys. The first time the kids sneak out, Granny is waiting at the bottom of the stairs, while the chains upstairs keep clanking.

Juno has sharp ears, Santi has an oil can, and Fritz has the loudest whisper in the world. To open that door before Mom comes back on Day 5, they need every trick they know: big sounds that hide small ones, one drop of oil, and a way past a cat whose bell tells on everyone.

Read the opening pages

Mom drove the way she talked: fast, cheerful, and with no brakes at all.

“Be good, be quiet, eat the stew, I love you,” she said as the car climbed Tumbledown Hill. “Brush your teeth. Do what Granny says. The phone’s in the study if you need me. And skip the ninth stair!”

“What ninth stair?” said Fritz.

“You’ll see, baby. Bye!”

Three kids and three bags tumbled out onto the wet grass. Then the car was gone, back down the hill faster than it had come up.

Juno Pickett pulled her yellow rain jacket tighter. It was too big for her. She wore it everywhere anyway, even indoors, with her red earmuffs around her neck just in case.

In front of them stood Gravelly House. It was tall and thin and crooked, with three floors and a pointed roof and windows like sleepy eyes. A stone wall ran around the whole garden, and in the middle of the wall was an iron gate.

Santi Reyes pushed the gate, and it squealed.

It squealed in three notes: low, high, higher. Juno heard every one of them, the way she always did.

“I have oil,” Santi whispered, patting the old bike bag clipped to his belt. He called it the Fix Kit. “One drop and that gate would never squeal again.”

“Don’t you dare,” said Juno. “Mom says Granny likes it like that.”

“WHY?” said Fritz.

Fritz was seven. He had a mop of dark curls, a gap where his front tooth used to be, and socks that never matched. He also had the loudest voice in the state, and when Fritz said “why,” birds left trees.

“So she knows when someone’s coming,” said Juno.

They climbed the porch steps, and Juno lifted her hand to knock.

The door opened before her knuckles touched it.

Granny Gravelly had to bend her head to look out of her own front door. She was the tallest person Juno had ever seen. Her hair was a cloud of iron-gray curls. Things were stuck in it: two pencils, a crochet hook and something that might have been a spoon. Round tortoiseshell glasses sat on her long nose, and she wore a mustard-yellow cardigan, a tartan skirt and green rubber clogs.

A brass ear trumpet hung on a cord around her neck, like a very small tuba.

“You’re late,” said Granny. Her voice sounded like a shovel in gravel.

“We didn’t even knock yet,” said Juno.

“I heard the car. At the bottom of the hill.” Granny stepped aside. “Shoes off. Slippers on. The slippers are not a suggestion.”

A card hung inside the front door, written in spiky black letters: GRANNY’S RULES. Granny held out a basket.
