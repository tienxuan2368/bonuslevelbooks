A Haunting Japanese Folklore Horror Novel for Ages 10-14

Read the opening pages

Your mother is at the back door, smiling. She hasn't blinked once.

In the mountain village of Yugure, the New Year ropes come down from every door on the evening of the dondo fire. That night, twelve-year-old Teppei Morita waves at a lonely figure at the bottom of the shrine steps and calls, "Happy New Year!"

The next evening, at closing time, something looks in through his mother's shop window. When Mrs. Morita turns around, she has a stranger's face. And the woman who steps in out of the snow has hers.

Now nobody in the village knows Teppei's mom except by her voice. Something with no face of its own knocks on doors at dusk, wearing the faces of people you love. The only rule: wait for the blink. A borrowed face never blinks.

Teppei, quiet Sumi and Wataru, the boy who records everything, have three dusks to learn what the faceless thing needs, before it comes for Teppei's face too.

A truly creepy, never gory novel for readers 10 to 14, built on Japanese folklore told with care: short chapters, real suspense, a loud boy who learns that the bravest thing is not to run, and an Author's Note on what is traditional and what is invented. For fans of The Mimic, the Roblox horror experience.

Read the opening pages

The New Year rope over the post office door came down with a soft crackle, like someone stepping on dry rice straw.

“Careful,” Mom said from the top of the stepladder. “It has guarded this door for three weeks, so say thank you.”

“Thank you,” Sumi said, and caught it.

It was a thick twist of golden straw as long as her arm, with white paper zigzags tied along it. A small bitter orange sat at the center, gone hard and brown. At the end of December, every house and shop in Yugure hung a rope like this over its front door. It kept the house clean and protected for the New Year. In some places people took theirs down on the seventh of January. In Yugure they stayed up until the morning before the Little New Year. Then the children carried every one of them up the Ninety-Nine Steps to be burned in the dondo fire.

Today was that day, and Sumi already had seven ropes in her arms.

“Eight,” said Teppei Morita behind her. He had four ropes over one shoulder and three over the other. His hand towel was wound twice around his neck, the way he wore it in every season. “That makes fifteen, counting yours, and I’ve had zero lunch.”

“You had lunch,” Sumi said.

“I had a very small lunch. It was a lunch you could fit in a pocket.”

Wataru Nishida stood a little apart from them, holding the microphone of his silver cassette recorder toward the ladder. He was tall and thin, with round glasses and a part in his hair so straight that it looked drawn on with a ruler. This was his first New Year in Yugure, because his family had moved from Osaka only a month ago, and he still asked why about everything.

“In Osaka we put ours in a bag at the shrine,” he said. “Somebody burns them, but not where you can watch.”

“Here you watch,” Teppei told him. “And while you watch, you eat rice cakes on sticks. It’s the best night of the whole year.”

“You said New Year’s Eve was the best night of the whole year.”

“New Year’s Eve has noodles, and tonight has rice cakes on sticks. They’re tied.”

Outside Okada Paper, Koto was sitting on the bench with a red cloth tied over her eyes.

On the bench beside her lay a sheet of thick cream paper. It had the outline of a round face drawn on it, and nothing inside the outline at all. Around it lay paper eyebrows, paper eyes, a paper nose and a red paper mouth. Koto’s mittens patted along the bench until they found an eye, and she set it down very carefully on the blank face.

For parents

Format
Tween thriller: 20 to 26 chapters, written for about a 4th to 6th grade reading level.
How scary
Scary, for brave readers 10 and up: dread that builds, no gore, and nobody dies on the page.
At the back of the book
An author's note that explains which parts are traditional folklore and which are invented, plus a note for grown-ups at the front.

A good fit for kids who are 10 or older and love ghost stories, folklore and Japan.
