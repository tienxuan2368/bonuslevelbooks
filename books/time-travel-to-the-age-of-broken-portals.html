Read the opening pages

Forty black gates on a hill of lava, one of them far too big, and the only key home in a fox's mouth.

Pilar Ibarra has never met a broken thing she couldn't fix. She has even drawn the plan to restore and light Dialwood's ancient Black Arch as a door to the Nether. Then she brushes a sooty gold nugget out of Tower Hill, steps through the Gate of Eight Clocks with Elias, and lands in the Age of Broken Portals.

Their biggest gate ever has just been lit, and zombified piglins keep stepping out of the purple. They leave you alone, until somebody hits one. Back home, the key is knocked from its frame, Cog the fox carries it into the forest, and the way home goes dark. Now Pilar must close a hungry gate in nine and a half heartbeats, while Anjali creeps after a fox who doesn't trust her and the sun keeps sinking.

Read the opening pages

Mr. Tolley measured the Black Arch twice, and it came out different both times.

“Nine knots tall,” he announced, frowning at his knotted string. “No, wait, it’s eight and three-quarters, so it’s shrinking while I watch it. Suspicious.”

Pilar Ibarra did not think the arch was shrinking. She thought Mr. Tolley had his thumb on the wrong knot, but she didn’t say so, because today she was busy being important.

She was ten and small, with two curly puffs of hair and a brown apron that had far too many pockets. The crew’s clock hung on a cord around her neck, and her compass lived in pocket three. In pocket one, the biggest pocket, she kept the most important thing she had ever drawn.

It was a plan for the Black Arch.

The arch stood where the mill road ended and the Giantwood began, and it was so old that nobody in Dialwood knew who had built it. It was a frame of obsidian, black and glassy, taller than two grown-ups standing on each other’s shoulders. A crust of netherrack lay around its feet, red and crumbly as old bread. Beside it was a bare square of dirt where a block of gold had once sat, until somebody’s great-great-grandfather dug it out and paid for Dialwood’s bell with it.

Every kid in Dialwood had touched the Black Arch on a dare, and every kid had run away afterward, screaming happily.

Pilar unfolded her plan and counted out loud, because counting made things true.

“There are fourteen blocks in a whole frame,” she said. “Four are missing, so those are the holes, and three are crying.” She pointed at each one. “That makes seven blocks to fix.”

The three crying blocks were the strangest part of the arch. They were obsidian too, but they glowed a soft purple, and every few breaths a purple drop slid down their faces and vanished before it reached the ground. Nobody could make a crying block, and nobody knew why they cried.

“Out with the spoiled ones,” said Mr. Tolley happily, “and in with seven good black ones. Bridget?”

Bridget Hourigan, the town smith, was leaning against the arch with her arms folded. She had a huge laugh and used very few words. Over her shoulder hung the only diamond pickaxe in Dialwood, the blue one that usually hung over the forge door like a trophy.

“I’ll cast the new blocks at the forge,” said Bridget. “Lava from my fuel barrels, water from the creek. Then I cut them out.”

“With the blue pick,” said Pilar, “because only diamond can cut obsidian. Anything else, and the block just won’t come.”

“That’s right,” said Bridget, and she almost smiled. “Your plans.”
