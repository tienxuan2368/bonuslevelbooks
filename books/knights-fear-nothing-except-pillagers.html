Read the opening pages

The realm's second knight just drank the dark bottle. On purpose.

Pebblecrown is the smallest kingdom in the blocky world: one castle, one village, one bell, one horse who only moves if you say please, and three squires who want to be knights more than anything. Loud Hildy wants to do every brave thing by herself. Careful Kiran knows every pattern a loom can weave. Gentle, enormous Alfie apologizes to everything and will not strike anything at all.

On Midsummer morning, boastful Sir Cuthbert drinks a captured Bottle of Ill Omen to win "a real deed" in front of everyone. The squires have one bucket of milk, one stubborn horse and thirty heartbeats to stop him. They are one heartbeat too late.

Now the first raid in forty years is coming: crossbow volleys, axes that knock shields aside, a ravager in the wheat, witches, and an evoker whose fangs bite up through the ground. Five waves. One bell. And a grand old Chronicler who can't finish a single heroic sentence.

A funny, action-packed quest novel for kids 9-12, set in a Minecraft kingdom where every raid, banner and iron golem works just like the game. Short chapters, no gore, battles against the game's monsters only, and a story about honor and teamwork: a knight is made by the people she protects.

Read the opening pages

Hear now the tale of Pebblecrown, the smallest realm that ever was, and of the Midsummer when its three squires learned how big a raid can be.

The kingdom was called Pebblecrown. It had one castle on one green hill, one village at the bottom of the hill, one river ford, one bell, one horse and a great many sheep. It stretched, in all its might, from the castle well to the village well. Its crown was carved from a single river pebble, and the crown sat on an empty throne, because King Hamund had gone fishing ninety years ago and was not back yet.

On the eve of Midsummer, the mighty realm was polishing its shields.

“For Pebblecrown!” shouted Hildegard Brask, and she struck the quintain with her wooden sword.

The quintain was an armor stand in old leather armor, with a carved pumpkin for a head. It wobbled. Hildy struck it again, and it came apart with a clatter and dropped its armor in the grass. For it is written in the laws of the world that an armor stand breaks after two quick hits, and Hildy only had one speed.

“That’s the third quintain this week,” said Kiran Mehta.

“It surrendered,” said Hildy. She raised her practice sword. “Justice is undefeated.”

Hildy was eleven. She had two short sandy-red braids that stuck out sideways, a face full of sunburned freckles, and a gap in her grin where a tooth was still making up its mind. Her leather armor was dyed crimson and gold. She was the youngest of six on the Brask farm and the only girl, and she meant to be the first knight the Brasks had ever had. Right now she was carrying three shields: her own, Kiran’s and Alfie’s.

“You can give mine back,” said Kiran.

“I’ve got them,” said Hildy. “Article the First. A squire shields others first.”

“That isn’t what it means.”

“It’s what it means today.”

Kiran sighed and turned back to the target block. He was eleven too, with neat black hair except for one tuft that never lay down, sharp eyebrows, and a stick of charcoal tucked behind his ear. His blue tunic had a pocket full of dyes, and now he raised the castle’s practice crossbow, breathed out, and fired.

The bolt struck near the middle of the target. At once a line of redstone dust running across the Tiltyard lit up red, block after block. It stopped two blocks short of the end.

“Thirteen,” said Kiran, and wrote it in his book. “Noted.”

This was the Long Wire. A target block sends a stronger signal the closer a shot lands to its center, and redstone dust carries that signal along the ground. A perfect shot lights all fifteen blocks. Kiran had never made one, so he wrote down every shot that wasn’t.
