Read the opening pages

Brush the dirt off a broken pot, and you can walk into the day it was made.

Elias Hourigan always jumps first and thinks on the way down. When he rushes a dig on Tower Hill, he loses a priceless find and crashes into a buried room, where a gate of black stone waits in the dark, ringed with eight gold clocks.

Now the town wants to fill in the hill forever, and the vote is in two days. With careful Anjali, clock-keeping Pilar, and Cog the thieving fox, Elias sets an ancient pottery sherd in the gate's one empty frame. At sunrise, the gate opens onto the Age of First Fires, when sniffers roamed in herds and people held back the dark with rings of campfires.

But the gate opens only at sunrise and sunset. Miss the turn, and you are stranded until the next one, in an age with no lanterns, where zombies, skeletons and one silent creeper come out at night.

Read the opening pages

Mr. Gaspard Tolley was measuring Tower Hill with a knotted string, and the hill was not cooperating.

“Twelve knots,” he called from the top of the slope, “and a half! Twelve and a half knots of wasted space.” He gave the string a tug, and it snagged on a sweet berry bush. “Progress!”

Down in the trench, Elias Hourigan pretended not to hear. He had a brush in hand, a block of gravel in front of his nose, and the sun still two hands above the spruces. Two hands was plenty. Two hands was practically forever.

The Dialwood Dig was not much to look at: three roped-off trenches on a grassy hilltop, a gravel heap, and barrels of brushes and buckets. A sign on a fence post said SLOWLY, SLOWLY in Mr. Tallow’s careful paint. In the middle of it all, poking out of the gravel like a giant’s elbow, stood the very tip of a tower made of glazed clay. It was orange and cyan, with a pattern on it like the face of a sundial.

That was the Dial, which was why the town was called Dialwood, and Elias had eaten lunch on top of it a thousand times.

The block in front of him was not like the gravel around it. It was grainier, with little dark specks in it, the way bread looks when somebody has hidden raisins inside. That meant it was suspicious gravel, and suspicious gravel meant something was buried in it, waiting to be found.

This one was also floating, with a thin line of shadow underneath it where nothing at all was holding it up. It just hung there in the trench wall as if it had forgotten how to fall.

“Technically,” said Anjali Rao, who was sitting on an upturned bucket beside him, “you’re brushing it from the wrong side.”

She didn’t look up from her Field Book, because she was busy drawing the block, and Anjali drew everything. Her long black braid was tied with red cord. When her round glasses slid down, she pushed them up with one knuckle without missing a line.

“There’s no wrong side,” said Elias, “just the side I’m on.”

“That’s exactly what a wrong side is.”

Skritch. Elias brushed, and a few grains of gravel trickled away. Somewhere inside the block, something orange caught the light.

“There’s a thing in there!” he said, scrambling closer.

“Two hands,” said Pilar Ibarra from the edge of the trench. She was ten and small, with two curly puffs of hair, a brown many-pocketed apron, and the crew’s clock on a cord around her neck. She held it up so he could see the little square sun sliding down toward the edge of its gold dial. “Two hands and a bit until the Hall closes. One, the sun is going down; two, Mr. Tallow says we stop when the Hall closes. Three, you’ve been brushing that one block since lunch.”
