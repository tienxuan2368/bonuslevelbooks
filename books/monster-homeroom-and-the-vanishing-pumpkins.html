Read the opening pages

Somebody is stealing the pumpkins. Except it isn't somebody. It's something.

Pearl Wispington is ghost-folk, a drama club star and the lead in the fall play at the Oddstone Playhouse. There's one problem. When she's nervous, she fades. When she stands near a soul lantern, she glows like a glow squid. So Pearl has a secret: a week before opening night, she quietly had the lanterns taken down from the dark space above the stage.

Now the pumpkins on Basil's Pumpkin Hill are vanishing and turning up in strange, high places. The whole town blames Principal Loftus, who is enderman-folk and famous for moving things at night. But Pearl has seen two violet eyes up in the dark, and they weren't wearing glasses. And the class parrot, who copies every monster in town, hasn't made a sound.

Four friends take turns telling one spooky week, with a fair mystery where every clue is on the page and a monster that follows the real rules of its world. An unofficial adventure set in the blocky world of Minecraft, with a minecart, a lot of rain and one very useful pumpkin.

Read the opening pages

Enter Pearl Wispington, center stage, holding a pumpkin. She is fully visible. The audience gasps.

That is how I would start the play, if anybody let me write it. Nobody does. The real script of Petunia of Pumpkin Hill starts with “Lights up on the hill.” Then there is a whole page of the Scarecrow talking before Petunia says one word.

Petunia is me, and Petunia is the lead, which I wanted you to know early.

It was Wednesday, after the bell, four days before opening night. The Oddstone Playhouse is my family’s theater, and we live in the house right behind it. My mother directs the plays, and my father runs the lights and fixes everything. I act, which is the most important job, and I would say that even if it weren’t true.

The stage that afternoon was the best it has ever looked, because of Basil.

Basil Bogwhistle built the Pumpkin Hill. It’s a giant step-shaped hill of grass blocks and moss blocks, thirteen blocks wide, with twenty-four pumpkins sitting on its steps. Twelve of the pumpkins have carved faces, and twelve are plain. Poppies and cornflowers and dandelions grow along the bottom row. Basil sat in the front row with his sketchbook, glaring at the hill as if it might fall down out of spite.

“It’s perfect,” I told him.

“It’s a hill,” said Basil. “Hills aren’t supposed to be perfect. They’re supposed to be load-bearing, and this one is, so I’ll allow it.”

“Do you like the pumpkins I picked for the top step?”

“I like that they’re square,” said Basil. “Square things stack. The whole world would be easier if more things stacked properly, including people.” He frowned at the hill. “There’s one moss block on the third step where a grass block should be. Nobody else will notice, but I will notice forever.”

Rufus Shuffleton was our stage crew. He was carrying a whole wall of scenery on his back, the way other kids carry a lunch. Rufus is the biggest kid in sixth grade, and the gentlest. He set the wall down without a sound, then stretched and groaned like a very sleepy zombie.

“Family thing,” he told Dorrit, who had jumped.

“Does it hurt?” Dorrit asked. “When you groan like that?”

“No,” said Rufus. “It feels like a yawn, only louder and longer.” He thought about it some more. “And with more of me in it.”

Lexi Plainfield sat in the second row with the whole script on her lap. Lexi is my understudy, which means that if I can’t go on, she goes on instead. She knows every line in the play, including mine, including the stage directions. Lexi is normal, which at Oddstone makes her the most unusual person in any room. She was also, I could tell, counting the lanterns.
