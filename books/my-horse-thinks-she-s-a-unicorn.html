Read the opening pages

My horse thinks she's a unicorn. My little sister agrees with her. I'm the one who cleans her hooves.

Ten-year-old Tansy Mayfield lives on Sunny Meadow Ranch, where every sunflower faces east and every chore comes before every adventure. Then a wild white mare appears at the High Fence, and the whole village starts calling her the Sunny Meadow Unicorn.

Tansy knows better. She's a horse: a proud, dramatic, apple-stealing horse who throws Tansy off again and again. Out here, a wild horse is tamed one try at a time. But Priscilla Vanderhoof, who rides the fastest horse in Cornflower Crossing, has sworn to tame the mare first, by the first full moon of summer.

And when the sun goes down on the Far Meadow, the night riders come out: zombies on gray-green horses, carrying iron spears, faster than any horse on the ranch. On the full-moon night, Priscilla is out there alone. Tansy's saddle is back at the ford. And the only way home is over the fences.

A daring ranch adventure on the plains of Minecraft, where every horse rule works just like in the game, and the danger is real but never graphic.

Read the opening pages

I was halfway across the barnyard with two full buckets of water when I saw her, and I dropped them both.

Cold water sloshed over my boots, but I didn’t even look down, because a white horse was standing at the High Fence.

My name is Tansy Mayfield. I’m ten. I have freckles and two sandy braids with yellow ribbons. I’ve wanted a horse of my own since before I could walk.

She wasn’t a gray horse or a creamy one. She was white all over, like a snow block with the sun on it, with a pale mane and dark, shining eyes. Grandpa’s peonies grow along the outside of the High Fence. She was standing right in the middle of them like she owned them.

The High Fence is the last fence on Sunny Meadow Ranch. It’s two fences tall. It runs from Lookout Rock all the way down into the Ribbon River, with one gate in the middle. Grandpa built it before I was born. On our side are the barn, the paddock and about a million sunflowers. On the other side is the Far Meadow, where the wild horses run, and wild horses never come to our fence. Everybody knows that.

“IMPORTANT!” My little sister Goldie came flying out of the house in her nightgown and her pink leather cap. Goldie is six, and she says most things in capital letters. “THE UNICORN IS AT OUR FENCE!”

“She’s a horse, Goldie,” I said.

“She’s the UNICORN from Grandpa’s story, and she came to live with us. She’s MAGNIFICATIONAL.”

Mom came out behind her with a bucket in each hand. Mom always has a bucket in each hand. She looked at the white mare (a mare is a grown-up girl horse), and then at my wet boots.

“Water first,” she said. “Then whatever this is.”

“But Mom, what if she leaves?”

“Then she leaves, and our horses are still thirsty.”

So I went back to the pond and filled my buckets again. I carried them to the cauldron we use for a water trough, slosh, slosh, cold against my legs. I fed and watered every animal on the ranch before I let myself go to the High Fence. That’s the rule on Sunny Meadow: water first, then hay, then hooves, then hugs.

The white mare was still there when I came back.

I walked up slowly toward her left shoulder, the way Grandpa taught me. A horse can’t see what’s right in front of her nose or right behind her tail. If you sneak up on her there, she gets a fright, and then so do you.

“Hello,” I said softly. “Steady on. I’m Tansy.”

One of her ears turned toward me, and the other stayed pointed back at the meadow. In horse, that means I hear you, but I’m busy.
