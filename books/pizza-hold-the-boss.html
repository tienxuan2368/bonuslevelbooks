A stranger just sat down in the boss's chair.

It's a packed Saturday at the Pizza Place, and ten-year-old Mae Galanis knows the oldest rule in the game: nobody gets paid until the pizza reaches the door. Then Chester's Wi-Fi drops for two minutes, and a stranger named BigCheddar takes the manager's Big Chair.

He sends every helper back to their own station. He hands out bonus checks to anyone who agrees with him. And when the kitchen catches fire, he sends Mae back to her register before she can reach the extinguisher. Two Golden Rushes are lost, the paychecks shrink, and the crew needs eight votes on a server full of strangers to win the chair back.

Meanwhile, Chester, who has never worked a station in his life, has to come to the Back Booth of Mae's family pizza shop for the first time and learn the Counter.

Read the opening pages

The last red booth at Galanis House of Pizza has room for five kids, if one is skinny and nobody minds elbows.

For my whole life, it has only ever held four.

On Saturday, four days after the cook-off, the four of us were squeezed in there with our reading logs. Booth Rule number one is homework first, even on a weekend. The rules are taped inside the booth on an order pad, in Uncle Stav’s capital letters, so nobody can say they forgot.

“Reading logs,” said Mom from the front counter, with the phone on her shoulder. “Twenty minutes. Then pizza.”

My name is Mae Galanis, and I’m ten. My family owns the Shop, which is what everybody calls our pizza place. I’m not allowed behind the real counter until I’m fourteen, so the booth is my office, and a pizza game is my job.

Emilio was reading a book about famous painters. He’s eleven, tall and skinny, with a short black ponytail and paint on his fingers from art club. He’s been my best friend since kindergarten. Every few pages, he stopped to draw on a napkin.

“Behold,” he said, and held one up. “Pepperoni, Resting.”

It was one pepperoni lying on a tiny couch.

Gracie was reading a book about race cars, and making the noises. She’s ten like me, short and loud and fast, with orange beads on her braids that click when she turns her head. She wears her big brother’s hoodie with the sleeves rolled up four times.

“Vroom,” she read. “Vrrroom. It doesn’t say that, but it should.”

Rafa wasn’t reading out loud, because Rafa doesn’t do anything out loud. He’s twelve, the tallest kid in sixth grade, and he wears the same gray cap every day. He finished his chapter, wrote 22 minutes in his log and lined his pencil up with the edge of the table.

That’s four.

The fifth kid in our crew lives two doors down, and he has never once sat in this booth.

“Managers work from the office,” Chester says, every time we ask him.

Chester is twelve. He has a neat side part, a backpack shaped like a briefcase and a clip-on bow tie he wears on Fridays. In the game, he’s our manager. In real life, his office is his family’s kitchen table, and he plays from there every single day.

“He’d love the booth,” said Gracie. “It’s sticky, and it has character.”

“He loves his chair,” said Emilio.

That was true. In the Pizza Place, our pizza game, the manager sits in a black chair in the office. We call it the Big Chair, because Chester sits in it every second that he possibly can.
