Read the opening pages

Every whirlpool starts with a stone. The Whirlpool Gap is full of them.

Atlas Dela Cruz draws maps of everything under the sea, and on his map one word marks the deep canyon past the Kelp Woods: DON'T. His grandpa Lolo has fished Barrel Bay his whole life and never turns down a bet. So when someone says nobody can fish the Gap anymore, Lolo rows out alone.

At sunset, the magma whirlpools pull his boat down to the glowing floor of the canyon. Prisma, a fearless young mermaid from Seaglass Reef, dives in after him and grabs him by the collar. Now an old man with one potion of Water Breathing and a mermaid with a brand-new shield are trapped at the bottom, and the drowned are rising out of the dark walls.

Atlas can't reach them. Swirl the nautilus won't go near the bubbles. But Atlas knows one rule of the sea: magma pulls down, and soul sand pushes up.

Set in the blocky seas of Minecraft and packed with real game mechanics (bubble columns, tridents, potions, and a lantern that keeps burning underwater), this fast, funny chapter book shows that even the strongest helpers sometimes need help, and that asking for it is brave. Short chapters, real danger, and a strict water-safety rule: a breathing aid and a buddy, every time. Real ocean facts in the back.

Read the opening pages

Atlas had counted eleven fish since sunrise. None of them was the right kind, and there was no school today, so he could keep counting.

“Cod,” said Lolo, and dropped it back into the bay. “Cod again. The sea thinks I’m hungry, anak.”

“You could eat it,” said Atlas.

“I could. But I promised you a pufferfish.” Lolo cast his line out past the end of the Old Dock. “A promise is a promise, even to a fish.”

Atlas sat on the last plank with his legs crossed, well back from the edge. Lolo stood beside him in his wide straw hat, the way he had stood every morning of Atlas’s life.

They needed one pufferfish. Just one. Then they could brew a new potion of Water Breathing. Atlas had drunk the old one at the bottom of the sea. Lolo always said a backup was the best thing in any boat.

The line twitched. Then it jerked.

“Hah!” said Lolo. “That’s no cod.”

He reeled it in, and up out of the water came a small yellow fish with worried eyes. It hung on the hook, spinning.

“Pufferfish,” Atlas breathed. “Lolo, don’t touch it with your hand.”

“I’ve been catching pufferfish since before your mother was born,” said Lolo, and reached for it.

The fish puffed. One heartbeat it was small and round. The next it was a ball of spikes as big as Lolo’s fist, and Lolo’s fingers were right there.

“Ow!” Lolo jerked back. The pufferfish shook itself off the hook, dropped into the bay with a splish and was gone.

Lolo stood very straight. Then he swayed, and his face turned the gray-green of old seagrass.

“Poison,” said Atlas. He was already on his feet. “Sit down. Lolo, sit.”

“I’m fine. It tickled.”

“You’re green.”

“I’m always a little green in the morning.”

Atlas ran. Forty-two steps down the dock and thirty more up the sand, and he didn’t count any of them twice. On the shelf in Lolo’s lookout sat a bottle of honey. Atlas grabbed it and ran back.

Lolo was sitting on the planks now, holding his stung hand against his shirt. He drank the honey in three long swallows. Slowly, the green went out of his face, and his own brown came back.

“There,” said Lolo. “See? Fine.”

But when Lolo handed back the empty bottle, his hand was shaking like a leaf on a windy day. He pushed it into his pocket fast, as if it belonged to someone else.

“Bet nobody in Barrel Bay can catch a pufferfish anymore,” said a loud voice.

Tucker Pike came down the dock with a bucket of bait, swinging it so hard that it slopped. He was eleven and the loudest swimmer in the village, and he had never once said a quiet sentence.
