A Funny Monster School Mystery for Kids 9-12

Read the opening pages

Somebody is stomping the turtle eggs. In broad daylight. And everybody knows nothing undead walks in daylight.

Rufus Shuffleton is zombie-folk, the biggest kid in sixth grade, and the hatchling guard of Hatchling Cove. This week, the turtles he walked to the sea when he was nine have come home to lay eggs of their own. Eleven of them. But every day at lunch, more eggs are gone, and the only kid ever seen on that beach is Rufus. Zombie-folk are famous for one thing around turtle eggs, and the whole town is whispering it.

Lexi, Pearl and Basil know their friend didn't do it. Proving it will take Lexi's light map, Pearl's best disappearing act, Basil's three red bottles, a very hungry cousin from the desert, and one long night on the sand, when zombies come in from the plains, something rises out of the river, and the eggs crack for the last time before sunrise.

Four friends take turns telling one wild week, and every clue is on the page. An unofficial adventure set in the blocky world of Minecraft, where every mob follows the real rules of its world.

Read the opening pages

Pocket count: one clump of seagrass, two wheat seeds, one carrot.

The seagrass was for the turtles, the wheat seeds were for nobody, and the carrot was for later.

It was Monday, before the bell, and the sun was coming up square and orange over the sea while I stood at Hatchling Cove. Hatchling Cove is the beach where the river ends, and I come here every morning before school, because I am the hatchling guard.

That is a real job, and Grandpa gave it to me when I was nine. There is no badge, but there is a lantern, and Grandpa says the lantern is the badge.

Six turtles were swimming in the shallow water by the shore, big and green, with shells like old pots. They were my turtles.

I mean, they’re not mine, because turtles don’t belong to people. But two years ago, when I was nine, I guarded their eggs on this beach for five whole nights. I watched them hatch, and I walked beside them, very slowly, all the way down to the water, until they swam away.

Turtles remember the beach where they hatched. They swim all over the sea, and then they come home, and mine came home last week.

Yesterday I waded out with the seagrass, and they ate it out of my hand. After a while, three of them swam up the beach and dug in the sand under the ridge with their back flippers. It took them a long time, and I waited.

Now there were eggs.

Three little clusters sat in a row on the sand, right under the tall yellow ridge at the back of the beach. Each cluster was a pile of small white eggs with green spots: four in the first, four in the second and three in the third.

Eleven eggs.

I counted them three times, from three different places, because a guard counts.

¶ ¶ ¶

Here is something about zombie-folk that people don’t like to talk about. I don’t like to talk about it either, so I’ll say it fast.

When a zombie-folk walks near turtle eggs, his feet want to jump on them.

Not a little, but a lot. It feels like a sneeze that lives in your feet. Real zombies have it too, but they just do it: they smell eggs from twenty-four blocks away and jump on them until they break.

I have never done it. Not once.

Near the eggs, I walk the slow way, with my knees bent and my feet flat, like when you walk past a sleeping baby. Grandpa calls it the sneak-walk. Sneaking never breaks a turtle egg, but jumping does, and so does standing on them.
