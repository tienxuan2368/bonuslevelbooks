Read the opening pages

A shore kid tames a nautilus so he can finally visit his best friend's underwater village. Getting there is the easy part.

Ten-year-old Atlas Dela Cruz has about fifteen seconds of air, and Seaglass Reef is a thousand strokes away, on the far side of the dark Kelp Woods. So he makes a plan: catch three pufferfish, tame a nautilus, and ride it all the way to dinner with Prisma's mermaid family. One pufferfish in three works. Atlas has exactly three.

But something is hunting in the kelp. The merfolk call it the Kelp Rider: a drowned with a trident, riding a nautilus of its own. By day it waits. By night it throws at anything it sees. And when the first rain of the season comes, the drowned can walk right up the beach.

Now Prisma, who hates sand and races dolphins, has to win the most dangerous race of her life. And Atlas has to learn that the best plans bend.

Kids will love Swirl the nautilus, a dramatic blue axolotl, a chase through the rain, and an adventure in the blocky seas of Minecraft, where real game rules decide who wins. Grown-ups will love the short chapters, two very different best friends, water safety shown without a lecture, and a page of real ocean facts in the back.

Read the opening pages

“Captain Brill! Is it true? Did something chase a boat?”

Prisma burst into the Rangers’ lookout with the question already out of her mouth. Behind her, two sea-pickle lamps spun on their strings.

The lookout was a tall tower of prismarine bricks at the edge of Seaglass Reef. Its windows were sea lanterns, glowing pale green. From the top, a Ranger could see all the way to the dark line of the Kelp Woods.

Captain Brill was floating by the big window with two other Rangers. All three held tridents, and all three looked tired.

“Patrol report,” said Captain Brill, without turning around. “One rider. Trident. Riding a zombie nautilus. East edge of the Kelp Woods.” He counted on his fingers. “One dry-foot boat, chased. Two dry-feet, ran up the beach. Both whole.”

Prisma’s tail went still. “Was one of them small? With a helmet that’s too big?”

“One was small,” said Captain Brill. “No helmet. The big one wore a hat.”

Lolo, Prisma thought. And Atlas. They had been fishing for pufferfish because of her idea.

“Both whole,” Captain Brill said again, more gently. “I counted twice.”

A slow trail of bubbles rose up the stairs. Gran Oriana came into the room, leaning on her old trident like a walking stick. Her silver hair floated around her head like a cloud.

Gran always knew when something was happening, and Prisma had stopped asking how.

“The Kelp Rider,” said Gran. “So it’s come up again.”

“You’ve seen it before?” asked Prisma.

“Not this one. Others like it. Every few summers, a drowned comes up from the deep water on a zombie nautilus.” Gran looked out the window. “A nautilus that has forgotten how to be one.”

“What does it want?”

“Nothing, minnow. It has no wants. It has rules.” Gran held up one finger. “By day, a drowned with a trident keeps it to itself.” A second finger. “At night, it throws at anything it can see.”

“And in the rain,” said Prisma, because every merkid knew this one, “they can walk.”

Gran nodded. “Rain gives with one hand, minnow, and takes with the other.”

Prisma knew that saying too. Rain was the best thing in the world. When it rained, her scales stayed wet on land, and her dry clock simply stopped. She could go up on the beach for as long as the rain lasted.

But rain also let the drowned climb out of the sea and follow anyone they could see.

“Two Ranger rules,” said Captain Brill. “Rule one. No dry-foot boats over the reef. Boats drop hooks. Hooks bring more boats.”

“I know,” said Prisma.

“Rule two. Nobody crosses the Kelp Woods after dark. Not Rangers alone. Not merkids.” He glanced at her. “Not dry-feet. Ever.”
