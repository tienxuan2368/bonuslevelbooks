Read the opening pages

A fox has run off with Beet's map. To get it back, the fairies must follow him into the snowy pines, where it is always winter.

Bluet is the smallest fairy in Dewlight Hollow, no taller than a poppy. Her friend Beet is the careful one: he counts everything, and he never flies past the edge of his leaf map. So when Rusk the fox trots away with it, Bluet, Beet and speedy Zaley follow him into the snow.

Then the snow begins to fall, and wet wings can't fly. The square sun is going down, a zombie is groaning between the trees, and something out in the dark is rattling like bones.

To last until morning, the fairies will need a wall of prickly berry bushes, one small light, and the oldest rules of the woods. And the most careful fairy in the Hollow will have to be the bravest.

Read the opening pages

Beet’s map was gone.

Every night it slept by his round door, rolled up tight. This morning there was only a little dent in the moss.

“It’s gone!” said Beet. “My map is gone!”

Beet had six pockets in his moss-green coat. He checked every one. One, two, three, four, five, six.

“Six pockets and no map,” he said. “Six is too many empty pockets.”

Bluet came flying over as fast as her wings would carry her. Bluet was the smallest fairy in Dewlight Hollow. She was exactly as tall as a bluet flower. She had warm brown skin, two round puffs of sky-blue hair and four white petal wings.

A little blue allay floated behind her, glowing softly. That was Chime. Bluet had given Chime a glow berry at breakfast. Now Chime followed her everywhere.

“Maybe your map rolled away,” said Bluet. “Don’t worry, Beet. I’ll find it! I’ll find it!”

She looked under the brown mushroom houses. She looked behind the old composter. She even looked in the Plant-One-More bed, between the firefly bush and the dandelion.

“Zoom first, think second!” Zaley shot past in a bright pink blur. She was trying to look everywhere at once, which is not quite the same as looking.

Have you ever lost something you loved? Then you know exactly how Beet felt.

His map showed every single tree between the Hollow and Plip Pond. There were eleven of them. Past them it showed the stream and the dark Pinewood, right up to its very last spruce. It had a pond in the middle, too, from the day it got rained on. Beet always said it was a very good pond.

Then Bluet noticed something red at the edge of the moss.

It was a tail. A big, bushy, red tail with a white tip.

“Rusk!” she whispered.

Rusk was a fox who lived in the Pinewood, across the stream. To a fairy, a fox is like a house with legs. He had a white chin and dark socks on his feet. And in his mouth, rolled up neatly, was Beet’s map.

“Rusk took it,” groaned Zaley. “Rusk always takes things!”

It was true. Rusk loved to borrow. He carried shiny things and interesting things around in his mouth. He hardly ever gave them back.

“Hello, little somebody!” called Bluet. “Could we please have our map back?”

Rusk looked at her, and his ears went up. Then he turned around and trotted off toward the stream.

“After him!” yelled Zaley.

They flew down past Duffle’s Digging Patch. They crossed the stream, where the water went plip against the stones. Then they flew into the Pinewood.

The Pinewood was a forest of spruce trees. They stood tall and dark, like giant towers. Ferns grew between them, and so did sweet berry bushes.
