Read the opening pages

The game room closes forever at the Ding. The power keeps going out. And the seeker is now a jar of jam.

Kenta Ward has never been found in a Showdown. Twelve times, Sol Benítez, the best seeker on Quince Street, has hunted him, and twelve times the clock has won. Tonight is a snow day and the last night of a winter event in the Roblox game Hide and Seek Extreme. The Holiday Living Room closes at 7:45: a giant tree full of orange bulbs that hide the seeker's glow, a toy train that whistles every twenty seconds, and five red stockings on the mantel.

Then the blizzard hits. In this game, leaving counts as caught, and every flicker knocks the hiders out. When the whole street goes dark, no Wi-Fi means no Showdown, and Kenta is secretly glad. Only he knows what in the basement could bring Game Night back. And if he speaks up, Sol's jam is waiting.

Four minutes. Don't move. Unless moving is the only way out.

Read the opening pages

The Golden Teacup had been on my bookshelf for eight Fridays, and it was starting to look at me.

It was a plain white teacup that Grandpa Tak had spray-painted gold. Whoever won a Showdown kept it for a week, and I had won the last one. After that, the game went eight Game Nights without making Sol It while I was hiding. No Showdowns meant no new winner, so the Teacup just stayed, shining between my sketchbooks and a jar of pencils.

That Friday there was no school. It had snowed all night, and Quince Street had disappeared under it.

(Size note: one snowdrift by our front steps = one Lupe, standing up, plus her hat.)

“SNOW DAY!” Sol yelled down the No-Peek Stairs. Then she remembered the rule about yelling and said it again in a whisper. “Snow day. Tonight is the event room, Kenta. Tonight is thirteen.”

Sol Benítez lived upstairs, and I lived downstairs. She was the best seeker on Quince Street, and in twelve Showdowns she had never found me. She talked about that number more than I did. Actually, everybody talked about it more than I did.

Outside, her little sister Lupe was making a snow angel. She wore her unicorn headband over her winter hat, because she never took it off. When the mail carrier waded past, Lupe sat up and told him, “Kenta has NEVER been found. Not EVER.”

“Good for Kenta,” said the mail carrier.

I went and stood behind the living-room curtain for a while.

At lunch, the lights blinked. It only lasted a second, but the whole house went dim, and the furnace in the walls stopped with a clunk. Then everything came back on, except the little Wi-Fi box on our hall shelf. Its lights had gone dark, and they came back one at a time, very slowly. I counted, and it took sixty-one seconds to wake up.

My dad, Hal, frowned at it, because he ran the Friday Server and the boxes were his department.

“That’s a problem,” he said. “The Benítez box upstairs is on Ruben’s work battery, so it never drops. Ours isn’t on anything. If the power blinks during a round, every tablet downstairs drops out of the game.”

Vera Goodwin had just come in from across the street, stamping snow off her boots. She carried the Showdown Book in a plastic bag so the snow couldn’t get it. Her silver whistle hung around her neck.

“Ref says leaving counts as caught,” said Vera. “Even when you don’t mean to leave.”

“That seems harsh,” said Dad.

“That’s the game,” said Vera. “I don’t make the rules. I just say them out loud.”

After lunch, I went down to the basement with Grandpa Tak. Little Quince lived down there. It was his model of our whole street, with tiny houses, tiny streetlights and a little train running in a loop. Under the table sat a battery box about the size of a lunch box, with two plugs and a little screen. Grandpa Tak had built it in the summer, and I had carried it down the stairs for him.
