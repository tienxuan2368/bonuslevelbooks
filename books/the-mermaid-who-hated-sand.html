A Funny Ocean Friendship Chapter Book for Kids 8-12

Read the opening pages

A mermaid who hates sand. A boy who won't put his face under water. And a treasure buried right where the sea meets the beach.

Every night, grumbling little gurgles sneak through the fence of Gran's Axolotl Nursery. To keep the babies safe, eleven-year-old Prisma needs a heart of the sea for a brand-new conduit. There's just one problem. The treasure map's X sits on the beach, under a whole lot of sand, and sand is the worst thing in the ocean.

At the end of the Old Dock sits Atlas Dela Cruz, a ten-year-old shore kid who counts everything, maps everything, and will not put his face under water. Not for anything.

Together they trade a lucky shell for a look at a map, sea pickles for a splash bottle, and secrets for courage. But when the gurgles come early and the conduit has to go six blocks down into the dark, both friends must do the bravest thing of all: ask first, and then help.

Kids will love the racing dolphin, the dramatic blue axolotl who plays dead, and an ocean built from the blocky game world they know. Grown-ups will love the short chapters, the warm friendship between two very different kids, the gentle water-safety wisdom, and a page of real ocean facts in the back.

Read the opening pages

Prisma shot over the reef so fast that the seagrass bowed flat behind her.

“Gran!” she called. “Gran, they’re back!”

Below her, the Axolotl Nursery glowed like a bowl of soft green light. Sea pickles sat in little bunches on the coral. Baby axolotls drifted between them, pink and gold and brown, each one smaller than Prisma’s hand.

Something else was in there too. Something sea-green and grumbling, something that should never have fit through the fence.

“Gurgles,” said Gran Oriana.

She rose up out of the dark, slow and silver. Her white hair floated around her head like a cloud on a swim. She leaned on her old trident the way other grandmothers lean on a walking stick.

“Three of them,” Gran said. “Little ones. They came in through the gaps again.”

Prisma looked at the fence. Gran had built it from coral blocks, with small square gaps so the water could flow. Each gap was one block wide and one block high, so a grown drowned was much too tall to squeeze through.

A gurgle was not.

Gurgles were baby drowned. They never grew up, and they never stopped grumbling. At night they came shuffling out of the dark water like tiny sleepwalkers who had lost their beds.

“Hrrmm,” said the nearest one, and it reached for a baby axolotl.

“Hey!” Prisma flicked her tail and darted between them. “Not the babies!”

The grown axolotls got there first. Axolotls couldn’t stand drowned, and four of them zipped at the gurgles with their frilly gills flared wide. The gurgles grumbled and backed away.

Then a small blue shape zoomed past Prisma’s ear.

“Wobble, no! Wait for me!”

Wobble did not wait. Wobble never waited. He was Prisma’s own axolotl, the only blue one in Seaglass Reef. A blue axolotl hatched only about once in twelve hundred babies.

Prisma said one was plenty.

Wobble charged straight at the biggest gurgle, and the gurgle bumped him with one lazy sea-green arm. Wobble rolled over, went stiff, and sank slowly to the sand.

“WOBBLE!” Prisma dove after him. “Wobble, no, no, no—“

“Minnow,” said Gran calmly. “He’s playing dead.”

“He’s not playing! Look at him!”

Wobble opened one eye. Then he closed it again, very slowly, as if he were taking a bow.

“He’s fine,” said Gran. “He’ll be up in a moment, and he’ll want a snack.”

Prisma scooped him up anyway. She was shaking all the way down to her fins.

By the time she looked up, the gurgles had squeezed back out and wandered off into the dark. Gran swam slowly along the fence, counting babies.

“Twenty-two,” she said at last. “All here. All whole.”

Prisma let out a long string of bubbles. “But they’ll come back tomorrow night, won’t they?”
