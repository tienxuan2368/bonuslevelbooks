Read the opening pages

Kofi Mensah has a plan. He is staying with his best friend's family for five nights, and by the end of the week, he is going to be a vampire.

There is just one problem. Mina Nightshade's family is night folk, and night folk are born, not made. You can't catch it. You can't practice it. And you definitely can't fake a sun-sneeze with a pinch of flour, no matter what the nosy neighbor collects in her bottles.

But Kofi tries everything. Beetroot soup for breakfast. Bat lessons with Mina's little brother, who keeps turning into a chicken. A cape that used to be a banner. And no sleep at all, because night folk don't sleep at night.

Mina should tell him the truth. Instead, she helps him hide a pile of pillows under his blanket, because nobody has ever wanted to be like her before. Then the phantoms come for Kofi. And one night, he walks into the darkest woods near Hollowbrook without a single light.

Read the opening pages

Nightshade Manor had not had a houseguest in one hundred years. It showed.

There was one guest room. It had one bed. The bed had one blanket, and the blanket had a family of spiders living in it. They had been there so long that Mom said it would be rude to ask them to leave.

“They’ll move,” Mom said. “Probably.”

“They’re very quiet,” said Dad. “Like ideal neighbors.”

I went down to the gate to wait, in the long shadow of the fence. The sun was setting behind the fields, square and orange.

My name is Wilhelmina Nightshade. Everyone calls me Mina. I have a black bob with one white streak, red-violet eyes, and a short black cape with a crimson lining. My hat is as wide as a cake. There is an iron helmet hidden inside it, because my family and the sun do not get along.

I held up my clock. It’s a gold disk with a tiny sky inside, and the sun and the moon travel around it. The sun was almost at the bottom.

Then I saw him, coming up the long path of Raven Hill.

Kofi Mensah. My best friend. My only friend, if you want to be exact, and I usually do.

He had round goggles on his forehead, a toolbelt full of redstone bits, and a bag over one shoulder. A tuxedo cat trotted at his heels: Sprocket, black and white, with a name tag Kofi made himself.

He was also wearing a cape.

It was black, much too long, and had clearly been a banner until very recently. He had dark sunglasses on too, though the sun was already setting.

“Mina!” he shouted. “Hi! I’m here! For five whole nights!”

Kofi’s parents had gone to visit his grandmother, far to the south, where the acacia trees grow. It was a long trip, and Kofi had school. So Mom had made them a promise.

“He’ll be safe, fed, and in bed every night,” she had said. “And home by the sixth sunrise.”

Nobody had asked what my family did at night. Everybody in Hollowbrook already thinks they know. They think we’re night people who run a bakery.

They are half right.

Halfway up, Kofi turned to wave. A lantern was bobbing along the lane below.

It was Mrs. Agatha Higgle. She is our nosy neighbor. Her spyglass hangs on a string around her neck, the way other people wear a scarf. A ginger cat walked beside her, wearing a name tag that said EXHIBIT B.

“Evening, dearies!” she called up. Her voice was sweet. Her eyes were not.

Since the Sunrise Parade, the Mayor wants Hollowbrook to be the best-rested village in the world. So he invented the Night Watch. Every evening, the Night Watch makes sure every family is in bed. Every morning, before sunrise, the Night Watch checks again.
