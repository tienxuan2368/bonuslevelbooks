Read the opening pages

His little cousin's very first full moon, and he just ran into the one place no wolf should ever go.

Shep Howell is eleven, a werewolf, and very, very fast. So when the whole pack treks three days south to the striped cliffs of the badlands for seven-year-old Tor's first Turn, Shep is ready to show him how a real wolf runs.

But behind the cousins' armadillo ranch stands an old mine framed in dark oak, and something has moved in. Cave spiders. Webs from floor to ceiling. The armadillos keep the spiders inside, as long as nobody runs.

Then, on the night of the full moon, Tor runs.

Now Shep has to go in after him, with his best friend Hana, his sister Maple and an armadillo on a lead. Charging won't work, because the webs catch everybody except the spiders. The only way to find Tor is the way Shep is worst at: nose down, slow, one step at a time.

Read the opening pages

By noon the spruce trees are behind us.

The road drops out of Howlwood and runs across a wide green plain, and the frost turns into ordinary grass. Noodle walks at the front with Hana holding her lead. The two Mori llamas follow her nose to tail, all by themselves, like a row of minecarts with no rails.

“That’s a caravan,” Hana says. “If one llama is on a lead, the others line up behind her. Mom says it’s the most useful thing about llamas.”

“What’s the second most useful thing?”

“They know what you are.”

Noodle turns her long neck and looks at me again. Her jaw moves. I get ready.

Nothing comes.

“She’s saving it,” Maple says.

“For what?”

“For when you have ears.”

That’s three nights out. Llamas can smell the wolf in a Howell from the moment the ears come in, and they spit at it. Ptoo. Every time. Right now I’m just a kid with a sword on his back and a scarf around his neck, walking on a sunny road.

It feels strange to be this normal. I keep trying to hear things far away, and I can’t. I can only hear the llamas’ feet, and Dad.

“Achoo. And the caravan takes the hill, folks. Achoo. Beautiful form from the second llama. Achoo.”

“Tomas,” Mom says.

“It’s the wool, Runa. It’s in the air. I can see it.”

Marbles walks beside Maple on his lead, very slow, very serious. Every so often Maple picks him up and carries him, and then puts him down again so he can feel useful.

In the afternoon the grass gets taller and yellower, and flat-topped trees start to stand around in it, far apart, like people waiting for something. Acacias. The sky looks bigger here, and the ground under the grass is dry and brown.

Maple stops walking.

She stands at the edge of the tall grass with Marbles in her arms and looks at it.

Mom stops too. She puts one hand on Maple’s head.

Pause.

Maple was born out here, on the last trip south, in the middle of the savanna in a hurry. That’s why her wolf is the only spotted one in our whole family, because a wolf’s coat comes from where you were born. Dad says she was “early, loud, and very spotted.” Maple says she doesn’t remember any of it, which is fair.

Okay. Back to the grass.

“It smells like warm bread,” Maple says quietly.

I sniff. It smells like dry grass to me. Five nights out, I’m the worst nose in the family.

“That’s how it smelled,” Mom says. “When you came.”

Maple gives one small nod. She puts Marbles down and walks on. I fall in beside her, and we walk a long way like that.
