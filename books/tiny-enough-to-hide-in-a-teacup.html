Read the opening pages

She finds everyone. He has never been found.

Sol Benítez is the best seeker on Quince Street. Kenta Ward is the one hider she has never found: eleven Showdowns, not even a sleeve. Every Friday on Game Night, the two best friends log in to the Roblox game Hide and Seek Extreme and become tiny avatars in a giant house, where a spoon is a bridge, a cereal box is a skyscraper, and the It glows orange while it hunts.

Tonight, Grandpa Tak has painted a teacup gold for the first Showdown Cup. In a warm-up, Sol finally gets one hand's width from tagging Kenta. Then the game makes her It for the last Showdown of the night. Kenta is the last hider on an open counter, the glow is coming, and the only way off is a bridge her Zoom can cross in four seconds.

Four minutes. Don't move.

And when someone breaks the most important rule of Game Night, Sol has to decide what a win is really worth.

Read the opening pages

The teacup on the middle step was gold, and I wanted it more than I had ever wanted anything with a handle.

Grandpa Tak had spray-painted it in the backyard after lunch, in a mask and gardening gloves, while Lupe and I watched. Now it sat on the middle step of the No-Peek Stairs, right next to his tomato-shaped kitchen timer. It shone like a real trophy, which was the whole point.

“The Golden Teacup,” Grandpa Tak said. His cardigan had about a dozen pockets, and he patted all of them like he was looking for a speech. He didn’t find one. “First Showdown Cup. The winner keeps it for a week.”

“I’m keeping it forever,” I said.

“A week,” said Grandpa Tak.

Sol Benítez, that was me. I was eleven that Friday, I was the best seeker on Quince Street, and I counted everything. The number that mattered that night was eleven.

Eleven Showdowns. Eleven times the game had made me It while Kenta Ward hid, and eleven times the clock ran out first. Not once did I find him, not even a sleeve.

Kenta sat three steps below the teacup, with his hoodie sleeves pulled over his hands and a pencil behind one ear. He lived downstairs, and I lived upstairs. Our house was so narrow that I could touch both hallway walls at once, and somehow he still found places to disappear.

“Tonight,” I told him, “eleven ends.”

Kenta looked at the teacup for a long time. Then he looked at me. “Be the furniture,” he said.

That was what he always said, and it drove me bananas.

“What does that even MEAN?” I asked.

“Nobody goes looking for a chair,” said Kenta.

“Everybody looks at chairs. People sit on chairs!”

“Not in the game,” he said, and that was the end of the conversation.

Fweet! Vera Goodwin blew her silver whistle from the bottom step. She had her composition notebook open on her knees, and on the cover, in marker, it said THE SHOWDOWN BOOK.

“Ref says rules,” Vera said. “The Showdown window opens when the tomato timer hits twenty minutes. After that, if the game makes Sol It while Kenta hides, the round counts. Before that, it’s a warm-up.”

“I know the rules,” I said.

“Everybody knows the rules,” said Vera. “Ref says them anyway.”

Every Friday, we played Hide and Seek Extreme on the Friday Server, which Kenta’s dad had set up just for us. The game picked a random room and a random It. The It played upstairs, the hiders played downstairs, and nobody ever peeked down the No-Peek Stairs. When the tomato timer dinged, Game Night was over, even in the middle of a sentence.
