Read the opening pages

Four nights to full moon, and the biggest wolf in the family just walked down off the mountain.

Shep Howell is eleven, a werewolf, and really bad at holding still. So when Uncle Sten comes down from the snowy Grove for the first time ever, Shep is sure he has found his hero: a lone wolf who needs nobody and never has to sit.

But a gang of armored skeletons is creeping uphill toward Uncle Sten's mountain. On the night of the full moon, Shep leaves his little sister behind and chases them all by himself, straight into the powder snow, where skeletons go in and something much worse comes out.

Now Shep is slowed by strange arrows and trapped on a ledge with a screaming goat. His wolf armor has cracked three times, and nobody is coming. Nobody except the quietest Howell of all, the one who has never once broken a rule.

Read the opening pages

The first thing I smell is snow melting in our kitchen.

It’s dripping off Uncle Sten’s coat onto the floor. He and Mom went back up the path with a lantern, and they were gone a long time. When they came back, Mom shut the door and pushed the lever down. Then she checked it twice.

“Well?” Dad says.

“Tracks,” Mom says. “Only his.”

“It stayed in the trees,” says Uncle Sten.

“What did?” I ask.

Uncle Sten takes a long time to answer, the way you’d take a long time crossing ice.

“Clacking,” he says. “Six. Gold on one.”

Mom goes very still, and I know why. We all do.

Two moons ago, on the night of the Moonlight Games, our pack chased a gang of armored skeletons deep into the Giants. One of them wore a gold helmet. That gang never went away. The gold one’s helmet keeps the sun off it, and the rest hide in the shade of the giant spruce trees all day and come out at night.

And now they’re walking north, toward the Grove.

“Supper,” Dad says, because Dad believes supper fixes most things. “Mushroom stew. Sit, Sten, please.”

Uncle Sten sits.

He sits in the chair.

Maple’s spoon stops in the air, and her whole face goes pink. For as long as I can remember, she has set a bowl at that chair every Moon Breakfast, with a spoon and a slice of bread with honey. Every time, the bread gets cold, and Dad eats it later so it won’t go to waste.

Now there’s a giant in it.

“That’s your chair,” Maple says, very quietly.

“Hmm,” says Uncle Sten. Then he says, “Thank you.”

Maple sits up so straight I’m afraid she’ll snap.

Uncle Sten reaches into his coat and sets a pair of leather boots on the table, next to the bread. Then another pair, and another, until there are five pairs, brown and stiff and new.

“Snow’s coming,” he says. “Boots.”

Dad counts them with his spoon. “There are four of us, Sten.”

“Five.”

He doesn’t explain. Maple counts the boots again, then counts us, frowns and takes out her notebook.

Maple has two notebooks. The one with the blue cover holds the Pack Code, the oldest rules of our family. The other one is brand new. She writes something in it and underlines it twice.

I try my boots on under the table, and they fit. Of course they fit. Uncle Sten looks like a person who has never once guessed wrong about a boot.

I watch him eat. He eats slowly and doesn’t talk, and nobody makes him. Nobody says, “Shep, sit still,” or “Shepherd, Code Four.” He just eats his stew in his own quiet, like the quiet belongs to him.

The Pack Code (one new rule per book) and Moon and Wolf Trivia.
